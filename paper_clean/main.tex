\begin{abstract}
% ============================================================
Electroencephalographic (EEG) representations of cognitive workload
exhibit substantial inter-subject variability, and population-level
models rarely generalize to unseen individuals without adaptation.
This paper proposes a Frequency-Informed Graph Neural Network (FIGNN)
that encodes EEG epochs into a normalized 64-dimensional latent space
by combining relative frequency-band power at electrode nodes with
frequency-specific phase-locking value (PLV) connectivity at graph
edges. A learnable four-band frequency-weighting layer modulates edge
attributes before two edge-aware graph-attention layers produce the
graph-level embedding. The model is trained with supervised contrastive
learning to organize the latent space by workload condition rather than
by optimizing a classification head. Learned band importances place beta
($w=0.364$) and alpha ($w=0.291$) as the dominant frequency bands for
workload-discriminative connectivity.

Transferability is assessed under strict 29-fold leave-one-subject-out
(LOSO) cross-validation. The mean Procrustes trajectory similarity
across the 29 held-out subjects is 0.860, indicating strong preservation
of the relative three-state workload configuration after removing
translation, rotation, and scale differences. The expected
centroid-distance ordering $d_{01}<d_{02}$ holds for 28 of 29 subjects.
A second-stage calibration experiment then examines whether the
transferred geometry can be personalized without retraining the encoder.
Subject-specific resting-state and known 2-back latent anchors are used
to estimate the unseen 1-back centroid, reducing the mean 64-dimensional
estimation error from 0.0723 to 0.0468 (a 35.36\% relative reduction),
with improvement observed in 22 of 29 subjects. These results support a
population-to-personalization framework in which a common workload
geometry is first transferred to an unseen subject and subsequently
adapted using minimal subject-specific information.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
EEG, cognitive workload, graph neural network, functional connectivity,
phase-locking value, supervised contrastive learning, transfer learning,
latent representation, subject-specific calibration
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Electroencephalography (EEG) provides a non-invasive, temporally precise
measure of neural activity and is extensively studied for cognitive
workload estimation. Workload-related EEG dynamics are commonly
characterized through spectral measures in the delta, theta, alpha, and
beta frequency bands, yet the same cognitive condition can occupy
substantially different regions of feature space across individuals
\cite{chikhi2022}. This inter-subject variability poses a fundamental
challenge: population-level representations trained on many subjects may
not transfer reliably to an unseen individual without some form of
adaptation.

A predominant approach in the literature formulates workload estimation
as multi-class classification \cite{cao2022,wei2025}. While classification
is useful for distinguishing predefined workload levels, it does not
directly reveal whether a learned representation preserves the
\emph{relative structure} of workload states for a subject excluded from
training. For graded paradigms such as N-back, where workload increases
monotonically with task demand, conditions can instead be viewed as an
ordered trajectory in latent space. Evaluating representation transfer
through latent geometry — rather than classification accuracy alone —
therefore provides a more principled test of structural transferability.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) offer a principled framework for
multichannel EEG because electrodes correspond naturally to graph nodes
and functional relationships between electrodes to graph edges.
Graph-based EEG representations and attention-based architectures have
demonstrated utility across a range of cognitive tasks
\cite{klepl2024,cao2022,wei2025}. Frequency information can be
incorporated at both the node level, as band-power features, and the
connectivity level, as phase-synchronization measures. Learnable
frequency-weighting mechanisms that adapt spectral emphasis to the task
have also recently emerged as an effective design choice for EEG decoding
\cite{ding2025freq}.

This paper introduces a Frequency-Informed Graph Neural Network (FIGNN)
that combines relative four-band power at electrode nodes with
frequency-specific phase-locking value (PLV) connectivity at graph edges.
A learnable band-weighting layer modulates the edge attributes before
two edge-aware graph-attention layers produce a normalized 64-dimensional
graph-level embedding. Supervised contrastive learning is used to shape
the latent space according to workload condition, with the aim of learning
a representation whose geometry transfers across subjects.

The primary evaluation question addressed in Stage~1 is whether the
resulting workload geometry transfers to subjects completely excluded from
training. A strict 29-fold LOSO protocol is employed, with a separate
model trained per fold and the held-out subject excluded from both
training and checkpoint selection. The representation is evaluated through
trajectory-level geometric measures — Procrustes similarity and
centroid-distance ordering — rather than through classification metrics.

Stage~2 then asks whether the transferred geometry can be personalized
without updating FIGNN parameters. For each held-out subject, resting-state
and known 2-back latent centroids are used as anchors to estimate the
unseen 1-back centroid by transferring the population-derived relative
1-back position onto the subject's REST-to-2-back direction.

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a frequency-informed EEG graph representation that combines
        relative four-band power node features, band-specific PLV edge
        attributes, and a learnable four-band frequency-weighting layer
        within an edge-aware graph-attention encoder;
  \item a strict 29-subject LOSO evaluation demonstrating that the
        learned representation preserves workload trajectory geometry for
        completely unseen subjects, with mean Procrustes similarity 0.860
        and 28/29 subjects satisfying the expected centroid-distance
        ordering; and
  \item a frozen-encoder geometric calibration procedure that uses
        resting-state and known 2-back anchors to estimate an unseen
        intermediate workload state, reducing mean 64-dimensional
        estimation error by 35.36\% without any encoder retraining.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Graph Neural Networks for EEG}

GNNs have emerged as a natural framework for multi-channel EEG analysis
because they can represent electrode relationships through a graph
topology that reflects the underlying neural geometry. A comprehensive
survey identifies graph construction strategy, node feature design, and
graph propagation mechanism as the principal design axes of GNN-based
EEG systems \cite{klepl2024}. Within the cognitive workload domain,
Cao~\emph{et al.} exploited functional brain connectivity to perform
multi-level workload classification, demonstrating that structured
relational information provides discriminative advantages over
channel-independent feature extraction \cite{cao2022}. Wei~\emph{et al.}
subsequently combined frequency-domain features with multiple connectivity
measures in a stacked graph-attention architecture (SGATCN) for
task-independent workload discrimination, representing the most closely
related prior work to the present study \cite{wei2025}. FIGNN
differentiates itself from SGATCN by (i)~introducing a learnable
frequency-weighting mechanism directly on graph edge attributes and
(ii)~evaluating representation transfer through latent trajectory geometry
rather than classification accuracy.

\subsection{Frequency-Domain Features and Learnable Band Weighting}

Spectral decomposition is central to cognitive workload analysis because
workload-related neural changes span multiple frequency bands, including
theta (4--8~Hz) and alpha (8--13~Hz) power modulation and beta-band
connectivity increases \cite{chikhi2022}. Fixed band-power features are
widely used for both node and connectivity representations, but they
assign equal importance to all bands irrespective of the task or model
objective. Recent EEG decoding work has addressed this limitation through
learnable frequency-weighting mechanisms that allow the model to
up-weight informative bands during training \cite{ding2025freq}. FIGNN
adopts this principle but applies the weighting operation directly to
frequency-specific edge attributes, enabling the model to modulate
connectivity information independently of node features before graph
attention.

\subsection{Phase-Locking Value Connectivity}

Phase-locking value (PLV) quantifies the consistency of instantaneous
phase differences between two signals and is a frequency-specific measure
of functional synchronization widely applied to neural EEG analysis
\cite{lachaux1999}. FIGNN retains PLV as the connectivity representation
across all four canonical bands, preserving their individual contributions
as separate edge features. Edge-aware graph attention then allows these
connectivity attributes to modulate message-passing computations
\cite{velickovic2018}, so that the learned representation reflects both
electrode-level spectral power and inter-electrode synchronization.

\subsection{Subject Adaptation and Resting-State Anchors}

Cross-subject generalization and subject-specific adaptation are
longstanding challenges in EEG-based brain-computer interfaces. Resting-state
EEG has been explored as a low-cost source of subject-specific information
for adapting models to unseen individuals, as resting-state recordings
are readily available without task performance \cite{an2024}. Individual
EEG measures also exhibit meaningful longitudinal stability
\cite{uudeberg2025}, supporting the use of a small number of subject-specific
measurements to anchor a pre-trained representation. Unlike fine-tuning
approaches that update model parameters, the Stage~2 procedure keeps the
FIGNN encoder entirely frozen and uses latent anchors to calibrate the
position of the workload trajectory geometrically — no gradient-based
adaptation is performed.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Framework Overview}

The proposed framework is organized into two sequential stages, as
illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:overall_pipeline}. Stage~1 trains a
population representation under strict LOSO evaluation and assesses
whether the resulting workload geometry transfers to subjects excluded
from training. Stage~2 keeps the fold-specific FIGNN encoder frozen and
uses two subject-specific anchor states to personalize the placement
of the transferred workload trajectory without updating any model
parameters.

\centering

\label{fig:overall_pipeline}
\end{figure}

\subsection{EEG Graph Representation}

Each preprocessed EEG epoch is represented as a graph
\begin{equation}
  G = (V, E, X, E_f),
\end{equation}
where $V$ denotes the set of EEG electrodes, $E$ the set of retained
functional-connectivity edges, $X$ the node feature matrix, and $E_f$
the frequency-specific edge attribute matrix. The final graph uses all
62 common EEG channels.

\subsubsection{Node Features}
For channel $i$, the node feature vector encodes relative band power:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_i =
  [P_i^{\delta},\; P_i^{\theta},\; P_i^{\alpha},\; P_i^{\beta}],
\end{equation}
where the relative power in band $b$ is
\begin{equation}
  P_i^b =
  \frac{P_{i,\mathrm{abs}}^b}
       {\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\{\delta,\theta,\alpha,\beta\}}
        P_{i,\mathrm{abs}}^k}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Edge Features and Connectivity Threshold}
Functional connectivity is computed within each band using PLV:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PLV}_{ij}^{b}
  =
  \left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
        e^{\,j(\phi_i^b(t)-\phi_j^b(t))}\right|.
\end{equation}
The four per-band PLV values form the edge attribute vector
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{e}_{ij}
  =
  [\mathrm{PLV}_{ij}^{\delta},\;
   \mathrm{PLV}_{ij}^{\theta},\;
   \mathrm{PLV}_{ij}^{\alpha},\;
   \mathrm{PLV}_{ij}^{\beta}].
\end{equation}
An undirected edge is retained between channel pair $(i,j)$ when the
mean four-band PLV exceeds 0.5. Each retained edge is added in both
directions to support bidirectional message passing.

\subsection{EEG Preprocessing}

EEGLAB recordings are loaded via MNE-Python. Channels outside the common
62-channel set are removed. A 50-Hz notch filter removes power-line
interference, and average reference re-referencing is applied. Signals
are then separately band-pass filtered into delta (1--4~Hz), theta
(4--8~Hz), alpha (8--13~Hz), and beta (13--30~Hz) bands.

For N-back recordings, stimulus annotations are used to extract
event-aligned epochs spanning 0--2~s from stimulus onset. Resting-state
recordings contain no task-aligned markers and are instead segmented into
non-overlapping 2-s windows. Resting-state epochs are retained separately
for Stage~2 anchor computation and are excluded from Stage~1 training.

The resulting graph representations are
\begin{equation}
  X \in \mathbb{R}^{62 \times 4}, \qquad
  E_f \in \mathbb{R}^{N_e \times 4},
\end{equation}
where $N_e$ is the number of retained edges after thresholding.

\subsection{Frequency-Informed Graph Neural Network}

The FIGNN encoder consists of a learnable frequency-weighting layer
followed by two edge-aware graph-attention (GAT) layers.

\subsubsection{Learnable Frequency Weighting}
Let $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$ denote four learnable scalar
parameters, one per frequency band. The normalized weights are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w} = \operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
\end{equation}
and edge attributes are modulated element-wise as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{e}'_{ij} = \mathbf{e}_{ij} \odot \mathbf{w}.
\end{equation}
This allows the model to up-weight connectivity information in bands
most relevant to workload discrimination without any manual band
selection.

\subsubsection{Graph-Attention Layers}
The first edge-aware GAT layer operates on four-dimensional node
features with eight attention heads and 128 output dimensions per head.
Concatenation yields a 1\,024-dimensional intermediate node representation,
followed by layer normalization, ELU activation, and dropout
($p = 0.3$).

The second edge-aware GAT layer maps the 1\,024-dimensional node
representations to 64 dimensions using a single attention head.
Layer normalization and global mean pooling produce the graph embedding:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}_G = \operatorname{MeanPool}\!\left(\mathbf{H}^{(2)}\right).
\end{equation}
The graph-level embedding is L2-normalized to produce the final
representation:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}
  = \frac{\mathbf{h}_G}{\|\mathbf{h}_G\|_2},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{64}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Supervised Contrastive Representation Learning}

FIGNN is trained using supervised contrastive learning \cite{khosla2020}.
For a batch of $N$ embeddings, samples sharing the same workload label
form positive pairs, while samples with different labels provide negative
comparisons. For anchor sample $i$, let
\begin{equation}
  P(i) = \{p : p \neq i,\; y_p = y_i\}, \quad
  A(i) = \{a : a \neq i\}.
\end{equation}
The supervised contrastive loss is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}
  = -\sum_i
    \frac{1}{|P(i)|}
    \sum_{p \in P(i)}
    \log
    \frac{
      \exp(\mathbf{z}_i^{\top}\mathbf{z}_p \,/\, \tau)
    }{
      \displaystyle\sum_{a \in A(i)}
      \exp(\mathbf{z}_i^{\top}\mathbf{z}_a \,/\, \tau)
    },
\end{equation}
with temperature $\tau = 0.1$. This objective directly organizes the
latent space by workload condition, without requiring a classification
head, and encourages workload-coherent cluster geometry that can
generalize across subjects.

checkpoint achieving the highest validation silhouette is retained as the

and checkpoint selection.

\section{Stage 1: Cross-Subject Evaluation}
\label{sec:stage1}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Strict Leave-One-Subject-Out Protocol}

Stage~1 applies a 29-fold LOSO protocol in which a separate fold-specific
model is trained for each held-out subject. For fold $s$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{train}}^{(s)}
  = \mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{D}_s.
\end{equation}
All epochs from subject $s$ are excluded from training, validation, and
checkpoint selection. Resting-state graphs are excluded from the Stage~1
training objective; the three N-back conditions constitute the ordered
workload sequence:
\begin{equation}
  0\text{-back} \;\rightarrow\; 1\text{-back} \;\rightarrow\; 2\text{-back}.
\end{equation}

For workload condition $c$ and held-out subject $s$, the latent centroid
is computed from all epoch embeddings produced by the frozen fold-specific
encoder:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{C}_{s,c}
  = \frac{1}{N_{s,c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s,c}} \mathbf{z}_{s,c,i}.
\end{equation}
The corresponding population centroid averages over the 28 training
subject centroids.

\subsection{Latent Workload Trajectory Geometry}

The subject workload trajectory is the ordered triplet
\begin{equation}
  T_s = [\mathbf{C}_{s,0},\; \mathbf{C}_{s,1},\; \mathbf{C}_{s,2}].
\end{equation}
Pairwise centroid distances are
\begin{align}
  d_{01} &= \|\mathbf{C}_{s,0} - \mathbf{C}_{s,1}\|_2, \\
  d_{02} &= \|\mathbf{C}_{s,0} - \mathbf{C}_{s,2}\|_2, \\
  d_{12} &= \|\mathbf{C}_{s,1} - \mathbf{C}_{s,2}\|_2.
\end{align}
The primary ordering criterion is
\begin{equation}
  d_{01} < d_{02},
\end{equation}
which holds when the 1-back centroid lies closer to 0-back than the
2-back centroid does — the expected configuration for an ordered workload
progression.

Trajectory shape is quantified using Procrustes analysis
\cite{gower1975}. The population and subject centroid matrices are aligned
to remove translation, scale, and rotation differences. Let $D_s$ denote
the resulting Procrustes disparity; the trajectory similarity reported
here is
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{proc},s} = 1 - D_s.
\end{equation}
A value close to 1 indicates that the relative configuration of the three
workload states closely matches the population template after rigid
alignment, irrespective of where the trajectory is located in the
64-dimensional space.

As a supporting orientation diagnostic, the 0-back-to-2-back direction
vectors are
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_s = \mathbf{C}_{s,2} - \mathbf{C}_{s,0}, \qquad
  \widehat{\Delta}_s = \mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{pop},2} - \mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{pop},0},
\end{equation}
with cosine similarity
\begin{equation}
  \cos\theta_s
  = \frac{\Delta_s^{\top}\widehat{\Delta}_s}
         {\|\Delta_s\|_2 \|\widehat{\Delta}_s\|_2}.
\end{equation}

\section{Stage 2: Subject-Specific Geometric Calibration}
\label{sec:stage2}
% ============================================================

Stage~2 evaluates whether the transferred workload geometry can be
personalized without updating any FIGNN parameters. For each held-out
subject, the corresponding fold-specific FIGNN encoder and normalization
statistics remain frozen throughout. The calibration procedure uses only
two subject-specific latent anchors — the resting-state centroid
$\mathbf{R}_s$ and the known 2-back centroid $\mathbf{C}_{s,2}$ — to
estimate the unseen 1-back centroid.

The process is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:stage2_pipeline}. The
population-only baseline takes the mean 1-back centroid of the 28
training subjects:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{pop},1}
  = \frac{1}{28} \sum_{r \neq s} \mathbf{C}_{r,1}.
\end{equation}

For each training subject $r$, the relative 1-back position along the
REST-to-2-back direction is computed as
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_r
  = \frac{(\mathbf{C}_{r,1} - \mathbf{R}_r)^{\top}
          (\mathbf{C}_{r,2} - \mathbf{R}_r)}
         {\|\mathbf{C}_{r,2} - \mathbf{R}_r\|_2^2}.
\end{equation}
The population coefficient is then averaged over valid training subjects:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{\mathrm{pop}}
  = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{r=1}^{M} \alpha_r.
\end{equation}

For the held-out subject, the calibrated 1-back estimate is obtained by
placing $\alpha_{\mathrm{pop}}$ along the subject's own REST-to-2-back
direction:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{s,1}^{\mathrm{cal}}
  = \mathbf{R}_s
    + \alpha_{\mathrm{pop}}\,(\mathbf{C}_{s,2} - \mathbf{R}_s).
\end{equation}
The actual held-out 1-back centroid $\mathbf{C}_{s,1}$ is not used to
construct this prediction; it is accessed only during final evaluation.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig_stage2_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{Stage~2 subject-specific geometric calibration. The
fold-specific FIGNN encoder remains frozen throughout. Resting-state and
known 2-back measurements provide two subject-specific latent anchors.
The population-derived relative 1-back position ($\alpha_{\mathrm{pop}}$)
is transferred onto the subject's REST-to-2-back direction to form the
calibrated estimate. The actual held-out 1-back centroid is accessed only
for evaluation.}
\label{fig:stage2_pipeline}
\end{figure*}

Population-only and calibrated estimates are compared against the true
held-out 1-back centroid using Euclidean distance in the full
64-dimensional latent space:
\begin{align}
  \epsilon_s^{\mathrm{pop}} &= \|\mathbf{C}_{\mathrm{pop},1} - \mathbf{C}_{s,1}\|_2, \\
  \epsilon_s^{\mathrm{cal}} &= \|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{s,1}^{\mathrm{cal}} - \mathbf{C}_{s,1}\|_2.
\end{align}
Subject-level relative improvement is
\begin{equation}
  I_s
  = \frac{\epsilon_s^{\mathrm{pop}} - \epsilon_s^{\mathrm{cal}}}
         {\epsilon_s^{\mathrm{pop}}} \times 100.
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Dataset}

The COG-BCI dataset contains EEG recordings from 29 participants across
three sessions and multiple cognitive tasks, including N-back, MATB,
PVT, and Flanker paradigms \cite{hinss2023}. The present study uses
N-back recordings for the graded workload trajectory analysis and
resting-state recordings (eyes-closed, acquired at session beginning)
for Stage~2 anchor computation. EEG was recorded at 500~Hz using a
64-electrode cap. Participants~1--9 lack the Cz electrode; the common
62-channel subset is therefore used throughout.

The N-back experiment provides three graded workload conditions: 0-back,
1-back, and 2-back. Resting-state is maintained as a separate condition
and is excluded from Stage~1 training.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Graph Construction}

EEG recordings are loaded from EEGLAB files using MNE-Python.
Non-standard channels are removed, a 50-Hz notch filter is applied, and
average-reference re-referencing is performed. Signals are separately
band-pass filtered into delta (1--4~Hz), theta (4--8~Hz), alpha
(8--13~Hz), and beta (13--30~Hz).

N-back stimulus annotations are used to construct event-aligned epochs
spanning 0--2~s from stimulus onset. Resting-state recordings are
segmented into non-overlapping 2-s windows. Relative band power is
computed per retained channel, and within-band PLV is computed for all
channel pairs. Edges are retained when the mean four-band PLV exceeds
0.5.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

All 29 subjects are evaluated individually under LOSO. For each fold,
normalization statistics are computed from the 28 training subjects'
graphs and applied to the held-out subject. The fold-specific FIGNN
checkpoint is frozen before latent embeddings are generated.

Stage~1 conclusions are drawn from Procrustes similarity and the
centroid-distance ordering criterion; the direction cosine serves as a
supporting orientation diagnostic. Stage~2 estimates $\alpha_{\mathrm{pop}}$
exclusively from the 28 training subjects; the held-out subject's
resting-state and 2-back centroids are used as anchors, and the actual
held-out 1-back centroid is accessed only to compute the final evaluation
error.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Learned Band Importances}

After training across all 29 folds, the softmax-normalized frequency
weights $\mathbf{w}$ consistently place beta and alpha as the dominant
connectivity bands. The mean learned importances are: beta $w_\beta =
0.364$, alpha $w_\alpha = 0.291$, theta $w_\theta = 0.178$, and delta
$w_\delta = 0.168$. The relative dominance of beta and alpha is
consistent with established associations between these bands and
workload-related synchronization changes \cite{chikhi2022}, and indicates
that the frequency-weighting layer captures task-relevant spectral
structure without requiring manual band selection.

\subsection{Stage 1: Transferability to Unseen Subjects}

All 29 subjects were evaluated under strict LOSO using fold-specific
frozen checkpoints. Across subjects, the mean Procrustes trajectory
similarity is
\begin{equation}
  \overline{S}_{\mathrm{proc}} = 0.860.
\end{equation}
The centroid-distance ordering criterion $d_{01} < d_{02}$ is satisfied
for 28 of the 29 held-out subjects. These results indicate that the
relative three-state workload configuration is substantially preserved
when the evaluated subject is entirely excluded from model training.

Fig.~\ref{fig:stage1_core} shows the per-subject Procrustes similarity
and direction cosine across all 29 folds. The majority of subjects
exhibit Procrustes similarity above 0.80, and direction cosines are
predominantly positive, confirming that both the relative shape and the
gross orientation of the workload trajectory transfer to unseen subjects.

\subsection{Representative Unseen-Subject Trajectory}

Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectory_analysis} provides a visualization of
trajectory alignment for held-out subject~17. Population and subject
trajectories are aligned in the full 64-dimensional space; the shared
two-dimensional PCA projection is used only for visualization. The figure
illustrates that the ordered 0-back, 1-back, and 2-back configurations

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Geometric Transferability}

The Stage~1 results provide evidence that the FIGNN representation
preserves a common workload trajectory for subjects completely excluded
from training. A mean Procrustes similarity of 0.860 indicates
substantial correspondence in the relative three-state configuration
after translation, rotation, and scale differences are removed, implying
that the learned geometry reflects a population-level workload structure
rather than a subject-specific pattern idiosyncratic to the training
data. The 28/29 distance-ordering result provides a complementary test:
for all but one subject, the 1-back centroid lies closer to 0-back than
the 2-back centroid does in the latent space, consistent with an ordered
workload progression.

This geometric consistency is distinct from conventional class
separability. The objective is not to demonstrate perfectly isolated
workload clusters, but to determine whether the relative arrangement of
workload states transfers across individuals. Overlap between
neighboring workload conditions does not contradict trajectory-level
transferability; it is expected given the inherent similarity of adjacent
N-back conditions and inter-subject EEG variability.

\subsection{Calibration Without Encoder Retraining}

Stage~2 further demonstrates that transferable geometry and
personalization are complementary. The population-only estimate requires
no subject-specific information and serves as a zero-calibration
baseline. The calibrated estimate uses only the subject's resting-state
and known 2-back centroids. The 35.36\% reduction in mean 64-dimensional
estimation error establishes that subject-specific information of this
limited kind can meaningfully correct the placement of an intermediate
workload state while leaving the learned encoder entirely unchanged.

The 7 subjects for whom calibration does not improve estimates are
informative. Geometric calibration assumes that the population-derived
$\alpha_{\mathrm{pop}}$ captures the relative REST-to-2-back placement
of the intermediate state with sufficient accuracy. Subjects whose
idiosyncratic workload trajectory deviates substantially from the
population template will benefit less from this procedure, and may
require additional anchor states or nonlinear placement strategies.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

Several limitations bound the current evaluation. First, the evaluation
is focused on latent trajectory geometry rather than continuous
deployment-level workload estimation; the relationship between
64-dimensional estimation error and real-world workload prediction
performance requires further investigation. Second, Stage~2 still
requires resting-state and known 2-back measurements from each new
subject; the approach reduces but does not eliminate subject-specific
data requirements. Third, because the latent space is 64-dimensional,
two-dimensional trajectory visualizations are projections: all
quantitative analyses are performed in the full latent space to avoid
projection artifacts. Finally, the evaluation is conducted on a single
public dataset (COG-BCI); validation on additional datasets and workload
paradigms is needed to establish broader generalization.

Future work can explore: (i)~additional anchor configurations and
nonlinear geometric placement strategies for Stage~2; (ii)~alternative
graph connectivity metrics (e.g., coherence, mutual information) in
conjunction with PLV; (iii)~extension of the framework to continuous
workload trajectories beyond the discrete N-back levels; and (iv)~the
quantum-inspired encoder extension as a comparative experiment on the
same LOSO evaluation protocol.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

This paper presented FIGNN, a Frequency-Informed Graph Neural Network
for learning transferable EEG workload representations and performing
subject-specific latent calibration. The model encodes EEG epochs as
graphs with relative four-band power at the nodes and frequency-specific
PLV at the edges. A learnable frequency-weighting layer — which
consistently places beta ($w=0.364$) and alpha ($w=0.291$) as the
dominant workload-discriminative bands — modulates edge attributes before
edge-aware graph attention produces a normalized 64-dimensional embedding.
The encoder is trained with supervised contrastive learning to produce
workload-coherent latent geometry.

Under strict 29-fold LOSO evaluation, the learned representation
exhibited substantial preservation of workload trajectory geometry across
completely unseen subjects: mean Procrustes similarity 0.860, and the
expected centroid-distance ordering $d_{01}<d_{02}$ satisfied by 28 of
29 subjects. A second-stage geometric calibration procedure demonstrated
that this transferable geometry can be personalized without any encoder
retraining. Using resting-state and known 2-back latent anchors reduced
the mean 64-dimensional 1-back estimation error from 0.0723 to 0.0468,
a 35.36\% relative reduction, with improvement observed in 22 of 29
subjects.

Collectively, these results support a population-to-personalization
paradigm in which a population-trained latent workload geometry is
transferred to an unseen subject and subsequently calibrated using
minimal subject-specific measurements, establishing a principled
separation between representation learning and personal adaptation.

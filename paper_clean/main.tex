\begin{abstract}
Electroencephalographic (EEG) representations of cognitive workload
exhibit substantial inter-subject variability, making direct transfer of
population-level models to unseen individuals challenging. This work
proposes a Frequency-Informed Graph Neural Network (FIGNN) that learns a
64-dimensional latent representation of EEG workload from frequency-domain
node features and functional connectivity. Each EEG epoch is represented as
a graph whose nodes correspond to EEG electrodes, node attributes encode
relative power in the delta, theta, alpha, and beta bands, and edge
attributes encode phase-locking value (PLV) across the same frequency
bands. A learnable frequency-weighting mechanism modulates the edge
attributes before two edge-aware graph attention layers produce the
graph-level representation. Supervised contrastive learning shapes the
latent space according to workload condition rather than optimizing a
conventional workload classifier.

Transferability is evaluated using strict 29-fold leave-one-subject-out
(LOSO) evaluation. Across the 29 held-out subjects, the mean Procrustes
trajectory similarity is 0.860, while the 0-back to 1-back centroid
distance is smaller than the 0-back to 2-back distance for 28 of 29
subjects. A second-stage experiment evaluates whether the transferred
population geometry can be personalized without retraining. Resting-state
and known 2-back latent centroids are used as subject-specific anchors to
calibrate an estimate of the unseen 1-back centroid. Personal calibration
reduces the mean 64-dimensional estimation error from 0.072319 to
0.046755, with improvement observed for 22 of 29 subjects. The results
support a two-stage approach in which population-level latent workload
geometry is transferred to an unseen subject and subsequently adapted
using limited subject-specific information.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
EEG, cognitive workload, graph neural network, functional connectivity,
phase-locking value, supervised contrastive learning, transfer learning,
latent representation, subject-specific calibration
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================

Electroencephalography (EEG) provides a non-invasive measure of neural
activity and has been widely investigated for estimating cognitive
workload. EEG workload-related activity is commonly examined in terms of
frequency-domain characteristics, including delta, theta, alpha, and beta
bands \cite{chikhi2022}. However, workload-related EEG patterns exhibit
substantial variability between individuals. Consequently, a representation
learned from a population of subjects may capture general workload structure
while still exhibiting subject-specific shifts in the location and geometry
of workload states.

Conventional EEG workload studies frequently formulate the problem as
classification, where the objective is to discriminate among workload
conditions. Although classification performance provides a useful
evaluation, it does not directly answer whether a learned representation
contains a transferable workload structure. In particular, workload can be
represented as an ordered progression of cognitive demand rather than as
completely isolated classes. A representation can therefore preserve useful
workload structure even when neighboring workload states remain partially
overlapping in latent space.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) provide a natural framework for EEG because
electrodes can be represented as graph nodes while functional relationships
between electrodes can be represented as graph edges. Recent surveys of
GNN-based EEG methods highlight graph construction, node representation,
and graph propagation or attention as important design choices
\cite{klepl2024}. Frequency-domain EEG features additionally provide
information about neural activity across distinct oscillatory bands.
Combining these forms of information makes it possible to construct a graph
representation containing both local spectral characteristics and
inter-electrode functional connectivity.

This paper proposes a Frequency-Informed Graph Neural Network (FIGNN) for
learning such a representation. The model uses relative band-power features
at graph nodes and frequency-specific phase-locking values as edge
attributes. A learnable frequency-weighting layer allows the model to
determine the relative contribution of the four frequency bands to the
connectivity representation. Edge-aware graph attention layers then
aggregate information across the EEG graph to produce a normalized
64-dimensional latent representation.

The main objective of this work is not to establish another workload
classification benchmark. Instead, the study asks whether the learned
latent geometry transfers to individuals who were completely excluded from
training. This is evaluated through strict 29-fold leave-one-subject-out
(LOSO) evaluation. For each held-out subject, the corresponding model is
trained only on the remaining subjects and subsequently frozen before
evaluation. The resulting subject-level workload centroids are compared
with the corresponding population trajectory using geometric measures.

A second objective is to investigate whether a transferable population
representation can subsequently be personalized without retraining the
graph neural network. Using the frozen LOSO representation, resting-state
and known 2-back measurements from an unseen subject are used as
subject-specific anchors to calibrate the predicted position of the unseen
1-back state.

The contributions of this work are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A frequency-informed EEG graph representation combining
    frequency-specific node features, PLV edge attributes, and learnable
    frequency weighting within an edge-aware graph attention architecture.

    \item A strict 29-subject LOSO evaluation framework that evaluates
    transferability through latent workload trajectory geometry rather than
    relying exclusively on classification accuracy.

    \item A subject-specific calibration procedure that uses resting-state
    and known 2-back anchors to improve estimation of an unseen intermediate
    workload state while keeping the learned FIGNN representation frozen.
\end{enumerate}


% =========================================================
\section{Related Work}
% =========================================================

Graph neural networks have increasingly been applied to EEG analysis by
representing electrodes as graph nodes and relationships between electrodes
as graph edges. Existing approaches have explored graph convolution, graph
attention, spatio-temporal graph learning, and task-specific graph
construction for EEG analysis and classification. A recent survey
systematizes these GNN-based EEG approaches and identifies graph
construction, feature representation, and graph propagation as central
components of the design space \cite{klepl2024}.

For cognitive workload, graph-based approaches have incorporated both
frequency-domain features and functional connectivity. Cao et al. used
functional brain connectivity features for multi-level workload
classification, demonstrating the utility of connectivity-based EEG
representations \cite{cao2022}. More recently, Wei et al. combined
frequency-domain EEG features with PLV, PLI, Pearson correlation, and
mutual-information graphs in a stacked graph-attention architecture for
task-independent workload classification \cite{wei2025}. These approaches
remain primarily classification-oriented, whereas the present work evaluates
the geometry of workload trajectories across completely unseen subjects.

The proposed frequency-weighting mechanism is also related to recent work on
learnable frequency selection for EEG. Ding et al. introduced an explicit
learnable frequency-weighting layer for EEG decoding, providing a direct
architectural precedent for adaptive spectral weighting
\cite{ding2025freq}. FIGNN differs in using frequency weighting within a
graph-based representation whose downstream objective is supervised
contrastive latent geometry rather than classification.

Functional connectivity is represented in FIGNN using phase-locking value.
PLV has long been used to quantify phase synchronization between neural
signals and provides a natural frequency-specific connectivity measure for
EEG graphs \cite{lachaux1999}. The use of attention-based graph propagation
is motivated by graph attention networks, which allow learned attention
coefficients to determine the contribution of neighboring nodes
\cite{velickovic2018}.

Subject adaptation provides another relevant line of work. Resting-state EEG
has been used as subject-specific information for adapting models to unseen
individuals, including resting-state transfer strategies for cross-subject
EEG decoding \cite{an2024}. Longitudinal evidence also indicates that
individual EEG measures can exhibit meaningful temporal stability, supporting
the broader motivation for subject-specific baselines
\cite{uudeberg2025}. In contrast, the present Stage 2 procedure does not
fine-tune the encoder; it uses resting state and a known high-load state as
geometric anchors within a frozen latent representation.

These studies motivate the distinction between architectural transfer and
geometric transfer. FIGNN is evaluated not only by whether workload states
can be discriminated, but by whether the relative arrangement of workload
states is preserved for a completely unseen subject and whether that geometry
can subsequently be calibrated using limited subject-specific information.

\section{Methodology}
% =========================================================

\subsection{Overview}

The proposed framework consists of two experimental stages. First, FIGNN is
trained and evaluated using strict leave-one-subject-out cross-subject
evaluation to determine whether workload trajectory geometry transfers to
subjects unseen during training. Second, the learned representation is kept
frozen and a subject-specific calibration procedure uses two known states to
estimate the latent position of an unseen intermediate workload state.

The graph construction and representation-learning stages are followed by
geometric evaluation rather than a conventional classification head. The
overall process can therefore be summarized as

\begin{equation}
\text{EEG}
\rightarrow
\text{graph construction}
\rightarrow
\text{FIGNN}
\rightarrow
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{64}
\rightarrow
\text{latent workload geometry}.
\end{equation}


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{EEG Graph Representation}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

Each EEG epoch is represented as a graph

\begin{equation}
G=(V,E,X,E_f),
\end{equation}

where $V$ denotes the EEG electrodes, $E$ denotes functional connectivity
edges, $X$ denotes node features, and $E_f$ denotes frequency-specific edge
attributes.

The final graph representation contains 62 EEG channels. Each node contains
four relative-power features corresponding to the delta, theta, alpha, and
beta frequency bands:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_i =
[
P_i^{\delta},
P_i^{\theta},
P_i^{\alpha},
P_i^{\beta}
].
\end{equation}

Relative band power is computed by normalizing each band by the total power
across the four retained bands. Thus, for band $b$,

\begin{equation}
P_i^{b}
=
\frac{P_{i,\mathrm{abs}}^{b}}
{\sum_{k\in\{\delta,\theta,\alpha,\beta\}}
P_{i,\mathrm{abs}}^{k}}.
\end{equation}

Functional connectivity between electrode pairs is represented using
phase-locking value (PLV). PLV quantifies phase synchronization between
neural signals and is computed independently for the four frequency bands
\cite{lachaux1999}. The resulting edge feature for electrodes $i$ and $j$
is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}_{ij} =
[
PLV_{ij}^{\delta},
PLV_{ij}^{\theta},
PLV_{ij}^{\alpha},
PLV_{ij}^{\beta}
].
\end{equation}

Only PLV-based connectivity is retained in the final graph representation.
Edges are retained when their PLV exceeds the final threshold of 0.5.
Correlation-based edge features were explored during development but were
not included in the final model.

Thus, the graph contains frequency-specific information at both the node
and edge levels.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Frequency-Informed Graph Neural Network}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

The FIGNN architecture contains a learnable frequency-weighting mechanism
followed by two edge-aware graph attention layers. The use of graph
attention is based on the ability of attention mechanisms to learn the
relative importance of neighboring graph nodes \cite{velickovic2018}.

Let $\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$ denote four learnable frequency
parameters. The corresponding normalized band weights are

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}
=
\operatorname{softmax}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).
\end{equation}

The edge attributes are transformed according to

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e}'_{ij}
=
\mathbf{e}_{ij}\odot\mathbf{w},
\end{equation}

where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.

This mechanism allows the model to learn how strongly each frequency band
contributes to the functional-connectivity representation rather than
imposing equal frequency contributions.

The first graph attention layer receives four-dimensional node features and
uses eight attention heads with 128 output features per head. Its
concatenated representation therefore contains

\begin{equation}
8\times128=1024
\end{equation}

features per node.

Layer normalization, ELU activation, and dropout with probability 0.3 are
applied after the first graph attention layer.

The second edge-aware graph attention layer maps the 1024-dimensional node
representation to a 64-dimensional representation using a single attention
head. Layer normalization and global mean pooling across graph nodes then
produce a graph-level vector:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_G
=
\operatorname{MeanPool}
(\mathbf{H}^{(2)}).
\end{equation}

Finally, the graph representation is L2-normalized:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}
=
\frac{\mathbf{h}_G}
{\|\mathbf{h}_G\|_2},
\qquad
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{64}.
\end{equation}


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Supervised Contrastive Representation Learning}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

FIGNN is trained using supervised contrastive learning rather than a
conventional classification head. Given a batch of graph representations,
samples belonging to the same workload condition form positive pairs while
samples from different workload conditions act as negatives. The supervised
contrastive objective encourages representations belonging to the same
workload condition to occupy nearby regions of latent space while separating
representations associated with different workload conditions
\cite{khosla2020}.

The temperature parameter used in the final training configuration is

\begin{equation}
\tau=0.1.
\end{equation}

This objective is appropriate for the present study because the downstream
analysis concerns the geometry of the learned representation rather than
only discrete class predictions.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Training Configuration}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

The final FIGNN training configuration uses AdamW optimization with an
initial learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and weight decay of $10^{-4}$. A cosine
annealing learning-rate schedule is used during training. The final LOSO
experiments use 500 training epochs and a batch size of 128.

A balanced sampling strategy is used during training. A validation subset is
formed only from the training subjects within each LOSO fold. Model
selection therefore does not use the held-out subject. The held-out subject
remains completely unseen during training and checkpoint selection.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preprocessing and Graph Construction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

The final preprocessing pipeline uses event-aligned N-back epochs rather
than arbitrary fixed windows. N-back stimulus annotations are mapped to
0-back, 1-back, and 2-back workload conditions, and each trial is extracted
from stimulus onset for a 2-s interval. This produces time-locked epochs
corresponding directly to the cognitive event.

The final graph construction uses the preprocessed EEG epoch to calculate
relative band power for each retained EEG channel and PLV-based functional
connectivity between channel pairs. Relative power forms the four-dimensional
node representation, while the four frequency-specific PLV values form the
edge representation. Edges are retained using a PLV threshold of 0.5.

The resulting graphs contain 62 EEG channels and four node features per
channel. The graph data also retain subject and session metadata required
for strict cross-subject evaluation and subject-specific calibration.


% =========================================================
\subsection{Stage 1: Leave-One-Subject-Out Evaluation}
% =========================================================

Stage 1 evaluates cross-subject transferability using 29-fold LOSO
evaluation. For fold $s$, all recordings from subject $s$ are excluded from
model training. FIGNN is trained using the remaining 28 subjects and the
resulting fold-specific checkpoint is frozen before evaluating subject $s$.

Formally,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{train}^{(s)}
=
\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{s},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{D}_{s}$ contains all samples belonging to held-out subject
$s$.

For the workload trajectory experiment, the resting-state condition is
excluded from training and the three N-back workload levels are evaluated
as

\begin{equation}
0\text{-back}
\rightarrow
1\text{-back}
\rightarrow
2\text{-back}.
\end{equation}

Resting-state graphs are retained in the graph dataset for the later
subject-specific calibration stage, but are not used as a fourth workload
class in the Stage 1 training objective.

For each condition $c$, a subject-level latent centroid is calculated as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{C}_{s,c}
=
\frac{1}{N_{s,c}}
\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s,c}}
\mathbf{z}_{s,c,i}.
\end{equation}

The corresponding population centroid is denoted by
$\mathbf{C}_{pop,c}$.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Latent Workload Trajectory Geometry}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

The latent workload trajectory for a subject is defined by the ordered
centroids

\begin{equation}
T_s=
[
\mathbf{C}_{s,0},
\mathbf{C}_{s,1},
\mathbf{C}_{s,2}
].
\end{equation}

Three pairwise centroid distances are considered:

\begin{align}
d_{01} &= \|\mathbf{C}_{s,0}-\mathbf{C}_{s,1}\|_2,\\
d_{02} &= \|\mathbf{C}_{s,0}-\mathbf{C}_{s,2}\|_2,\\
d_{12} &= \|\mathbf{C}_{s,1}-\mathbf{C}_{s,2}\|_2.
\end{align}

A basic monotonicity criterion tests whether

\begin{equation}
d_{01}<d_{02}.
\end{equation}

This asks whether the highest workload state is farther from the 0-back
state than the intermediate 1-back state.

Trajectory similarity between the held-out subject and population
trajectories is additionally quantified using Procrustes alignment.
Generalized Procrustes analysis removes differences in translation,
rotation, and scale and therefore focuses on the relative configuration of
the workload states \cite{gower1975}.


% =========================================================
\subsection{Stage 2: Subject-Specific Calibration}
% =========================================================

Stage 2 investigates whether transferable population geometry can be
personalized without retraining FIGNN.

For each held-out subject, the corresponding LOSO model remains frozen. The
subject-specific resting-state and known 2-back latent centroids are used as
two personal anchors. The target is the subject's 1-back latent centroid,
which is treated as an unseen intermediate state during the calibration
procedure.

The population-only method provides a baseline estimate of the subject's
1-back position using population trajectory information. The calibrated
method transforms this estimate using the subject-specific anchor
configuration.

Let

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{s,1}^{pop}
\end{equation}

denote the population-only estimate and

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{s,1}^{cal}
\end{equation}

denote the calibrated estimate.

Both are compared against the actual held-out subject 1-back centroid
$\mathbf{C}_{s,1}$ using the 64-dimensional Euclidean error:

\begin{align}
\epsilon_s^{pop}
&=
\left\|
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{s,1}^{pop}
-
\mathbf{C}_{s,1}
\right\|_2,\\
\epsilon_s^{cal}
&=
\left\|
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{s,1}^{cal}
-
\mathbf{C}_{s,1}
\right\|_2.
\end{align}

The subject-level percentage improvement is

\begin{equation}
I_s
=
\frac{
\epsilon_s^{pop}
-
\epsilon_s^{cal}
}{
\epsilon_s^{pop}
}
\times100.
\end{equation}

No FIGNN parameters are updated using the held-out subject. Calibration
therefore operates in the frozen latent representation rather than through
subject-specific model retraining.


% =========================================================
\section{Experimental Setup}
% =========================================================

\subsection{Dataset}

The primary dataset used in this study is the COG-BCI database
\cite{hinss2023}. It contains recordings from 29 participants across three
sessions, with the sessions spaced one week apart. The dataset includes
multiple cognitive tasks, including N-back, MATB, PVT, and Flanker, as well
as resting-state recordings. The present work uses the N-back conditions
for the graded workload trajectory analysis. The dataset documentation
reports three blocks each of 0-back, 1-back, and 2-back per session.

The COG-BCI recordings were acquired using a 64-electrode EEG system at
500 Hz. For participants 1--9, electrode Cz was not recorded and is
therefore absent from those datasets. After the final preprocessing and
channel-selection pipeline, the graph representation uses 62 EEG channels.

Importantly, resting state is treated separately from the N-back workload
conditions. It is retained in the processed graph collection for the
subject-specific Stage 2 calibration experiment, but it is not treated as a
fourth workload level in Stage 1.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Preprocessing and Graph Construction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

N-back stimulus annotations are used to construct event-aligned epochs.
The final event mapping assigns the N-back stimulus events to the three
workload conditions and extracts a 0--2 s interval following stimulus onset.
This ensures that the extracted signal is aligned with the corresponding
cognitive event rather than being divided into arbitrary fixed windows.

For every retained EEG channel, relative power is computed for the four
frequency bands used by FIGNN. The resulting four-dimensional vectors form
the node features. PLV is computed between electrode pairs independently
for the same four bands. An edge is retained when its PLV exceeds 0.5,
producing a sparse functional-connectivity graph.

The final graph therefore has the form

\begin{equation}
X\in\mathbb{R}^{62\times4},
\qquad
E_f\in\mathbb{R}^{N_e\times4},
\end{equation}

where $N_e$ is the number of retained functional-connectivity edges for a
given epoch.

The processed graphs contain subject and session identifiers so that
subject-level separation can be enforced during LOSO evaluation.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

All 29 subjects are evaluated individually under LOSO. For every fold, the
model trained on the remaining subjects is frozen before latent embeddings
are generated for the held-out subject.

The primary Stage 1 metrics are trajectory-level geometric measures,
particularly Procrustes similarity and the ordering of centroid distances.
Additional diagnostics are retained as supporting analyses rather than
treated as the primary evidence for transferability.

Stage 2 evaluates whether subject-specific calibration reduces 1-back
estimation error relative to the population-only baseline. All quantitative
distance and calibration calculations are performed in the original
64-dimensional latent space.


% =========================================================
\section{Results}
% =========================================================

\subsection{Stage 1: Transferability to Unseen Subjects}

All 29 subjects were evaluated under strict LOSO evaluation using
fold-specific frozen checkpoints.

Across subjects, the mean Procrustes trajectory similarity was

\begin{equation}
\bar{S}_{proc}=0.860.
\end{equation}

Furthermore, the monotonic distance relationship

\begin{equation}
d_{01}<d_{02}
\end{equation}

was observed for 28 of the 29 held-out subjects.

These results indicate that the relative geometry of the workload trajectory
is substantially preserved across subjects even though the individual
workload representations are not perfectly separated into isolated
clusters.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stage 2: Personal Calibration}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

The Stage 2 experiment evaluates whether the population representation can
be personalized using resting-state and known 2-back anchors while keeping
the FIGNN model frozen.

Across the 29 subjects, the mean population-only 64-dimensional estimation
error for the unseen 1-back centroid was

\begin{equation}
\bar{\epsilon}^{pop}=0.072319,
\end{equation}

whereas the mean calibrated error was

\begin{equation}
\bar{\epsilon}^{cal}=0.046755.
\end{equation}

The corresponding mean absolute reduction in error was

\begin{equation}
0.072319-0.046755=0.025564.
\end{equation}

This corresponds to a mean relative reduction of approximately 24.06\%.
Calibration improved the estimation for 22 of the 29 subjects.

The result demonstrates that transferability of the population geometry and
personalization are complementary. The population representation provides a
common workload structure, while subject-specific information can correct
displacement of that structure in an unseen individual's latent space.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Discussion}
% =========================================================

The results support a representation-learning view of EEG cognitive
workload in which transferability does not necessarily require complete
class separation. FIGNN produces a latent representation in which the
relative arrangement of workload states is substantially preserved across
unseen subjects, despite overlap between individual states.

The mean Procrustes similarity of approximately 0.86 provides evidence that
the three-state trajectory configuration is reasonably stable across
subjects after removal of translation, scale, and rotation. The fact that
28 of 29 subjects satisfy $d_{01}<d_{02}$ provides an additional
interpretable indication that the latent distance from the 0-back state
generally increases with workload.

At the same time, the low silhouette values observed in the broader
evaluation demonstrate that the latent representation should not be
characterized as a conventional well-separated three-class embedding. The
representation can preserve an ordered workload trajectory even when
samples belonging to neighboring workload conditions overlap.

The Stage 2 experiment provides a complementary result. A population model
can establish a transferable workload geometry, but the absolute latent
location of that geometry can differ for a new individual. Rather than
retraining FIGNN, the proposed calibration uses subject-specific anchors to
adapt the population-level estimate. The reduction from 0.072319 to 0.046755
in mean 64-dimensional estimation error indicates that this geometric
adaptation can recover a substantial portion of the subject-specific
displacement.

The combination of the two stages therefore suggests a practical
population-to-personalization workflow. Population-level representation
learning establishes a common workload geometry, while limited
subject-specific measurements provide anchors for adapting that geometry to
an unseen individual.


% =========================================================
\section{Limitations}
% =========================================================

Several limitations should be considered.

First, the present study evaluates transferability using latent geometric
structure rather than demonstrating deployment-level continuous workload
estimation.

Second, the calibration experiment assumes that resting-state and 2-back
measurements are available for the new subject. The method therefore
reduces, rather than eliminates, the requirement for subject-specific
calibration data.

Third, the latent representation is 64-dimensional and the two-dimensional
trajectory visualizations are projections. Consequently, visual
relationships in PCA plots should not be interpreted as quantitative
evidence about the full latent space. All quantitative distance and
calibration analyses are performed in the original 64-dimensional space.

Fourth, the Stage 1 silhouette results indicate that the learned
representation does not produce perfectly separated workload clusters.
The contribution of FIGNN should therefore be interpreted primarily in terms
of transferable workload geometry rather than superior classification
separability.

Finally, the current experiments are based on a single primary dataset and
should be validated on additional EEG workload datasets and experimental
protocols to establish broader generalizability.


% =========================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =========================================================

This paper presented FIGNN, a Frequency-Informed Graph Neural Network for
learning transferable EEG workload representations and performing
subject-specific latent calibration. The model combines relative
frequency-band power at EEG electrodes with frequency-specific PLV
connectivity, learnable frequency weighting, and edge-aware graph attention
to produce a normalized 64-dimensional latent representation.

Using strict 29-fold LOSO evaluation, the learned representation exhibited
substantial preservation of workload trajectory geometry across unseen
subjects. The mean Procrustes similarity was approximately 0.860, and
28 of 29 subjects showed the expected monotonic relationship between
0-back, 1-back, and 2-back centroid distances.

A second calibration stage demonstrated that this transferable geometry can
be personalized without retraining the FIGNN model. Using resting-state and
known 2-back anchors reduced the mean unseen 1-back estimation error from
0.072319 to 0.046755, with improvement observed in 22 of 29 subjects.

These findings support a two-stage paradigm for EEG workload
representation: first learn a population-level latent geometry that
transfers across subjects, and then use limited subject-specific anchors to
calibrate that geometry for a new individual.
